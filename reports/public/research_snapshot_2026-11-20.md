# Research Snapshot: Energy-Guided Weight-Space Repair

Date: 2026-11-20

Status: pilot. Tables are generated by `examples/run_energy_repair.py`
(schema `energy_repair/v2`, seeds 0-19) into `outputs/public/energy_repair/`.

## Question

Can an energy model guide a lasting edit to a policy's weights: high energy
marking what needs repair, and low-energy regions marking what must not move?

## Result

When every method is told which layout is damaged, energy-guided edits
recovered 96-101% of the return lost to the planted fault, and 0.4-1.8% of
held-out positions on the other layouts lost more than 0.10 return at the
worst point of the edit path. The position-blind control recovered 79-87% with
5.5-9.7% peak harm. Selecting edits for repair alone gave 17-24% peak harm.

Rotating the weight coordinates kept recovery at 103-105% but raised
energy-guided peak harm to 6.4-7.8%. The position-blind control under rotation
recovered 83-88% with 15-16% peak harm.

The energy model that guides the repair is itself a better policy than the
selector, so this shows energy-guided distillation into weights, not repair
from a weaker signal (caveat 1).

## Setup

- **Positions.** All 9,480 non-terminal (attacker cell, defender cell) starts
  on the five Gridworld v2 layouts.
- **Returns.** Exact. Each of the five named attacker strategies is rolled out
  to termination from each position against each of the five scripted
  defenders, and the return is the mean over defenders.
- **Selector.** A 3,413-weight MLP mapping position features plus a layout
  one-hot to a mix over strategies. A position's return is the mix-weighted
  strategy return.
- **Energy.** `EnergyMLP` over (position, strategy embedding), trained to rank
  strategies by return. The energy of a decision is its expected excess over
  the lowest-energy strategy at that position.
- **Split, per seed.** 70% of positions fit the selector and the energy, 10%
  are the evidence the search sees, 20% are held out for every number below.
- **Faults.** Fine-tune all weights so the selector overuses one strategy on
  one layout (layout = seed mod 5). `blanket` forces it at every position;
  `selective` only where that strategy loses more than 0.10 return.
  `aggressive` is almost never uniquely right; `feint` is uniquely right at
  roughly 15% of positions. Both were chosen from layout statistics before any
  repair was run.
- **Search.** 40 steps; each step edits 32 weight coordinates and picks a step
  size from a fixed grid. The evidence positions with the highest decision
  energy are flagged for repair; the rest are protected.
- **Flagging scope.** `global` flags the top quarter of all evidence positions
  and tells the method nothing about where the fault is. `fault_layout` tells
  every method which layout is damaged and flags the top half of that layout
  only, so no method is forced to edit the other layouts.
- **Rotation.** A random orthogonal change of weight coordinates. The policy
  is identical, but no coordinate belongs to a single layout any more.

| Method | Guidance signal | Protects unflagged positions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `energy_guided` | learned energy | yes |
| `repair_only` | learned energy | no |
| `position_blind` | learned energy averaged over positions (one score per strategy) | yes |
| `oracle_regret` | true regret, in energy units | yes |

return lost to the fault. Peak harm is the largest share, over the 41 points
of the edit path, of other-layout held-out positions that lost more than 0.10
return relative to the damaged selector; ± is a 95% CI half-width over seeds.

## Reading the controls

- **Position-blind.** With the flagging quota removed, the blind control
  still recovers 12-20 points less and does 5-14 times the harm in original
  coordinates. It moves flagged positions to the on-average-best strategy, and
  it cannot see harm: its preservation term only resists moves toward
  strategies that are worse on average.
- **Sharper fault.** `feint/selective` is the case where the overused strategy
  is right at some positions and wrong at others. Energy-guided: 100.4%
  recovery, 1.81% peak harm. Position-blind: 83.1%, 9.65%.
- **Rotation.** In original coordinates the scoped search finds edits that
  barely touch other layouts (0.4-1.8%). After rotation no such coordinates
  exist and peak harm is 3.5-17 times higher. Recovery is unaffected.
- **Oracle.** True regret gives 0.1-0.6% peak harm with scoped flagging in
  original coordinates, so part of the energy-guided harm there is energy
  error. After rotation the oracle is at 4.8-5.8%, so most of that harm comes
  from the coordinates, not the signal.

## Caveats

1. **The energy model is a better policy than the selector.** Acting on the
   lowest-energy strategy scores 0.700 on held-out positions (seeds 0-4); the
   clean selector scores 0.663 and the per-position best is 0.735. The energy
   was trained on exact returns for 70% of positions, including the faulted
   layout. The repair is therefore distillation from an intact teacher into a
   damaged student through sparse edits. Recovery above 100% is the same fact.
2. **Preservation is expressed in energy.** A penalty on behaviour change at
   unflagged positions needs no energy model and was not run. It would likely
   cut the position-blind control's harm; it would not change its recovery.
3. **Scoped flagging assumes the damaged layout is known.** Under global
   flagging the search also edits other layouts where the clean selector was
   already wrong, so harm there is not purely collateral.
4. **Protocol changes after first results.** Dev seeds 100-103 shaped two
   choices before seeds 0-19 were first run (decision energy as excess over the
   per-position minimum; a quadratic preservation penalty). After that run the
   scoped flagging arms were added and the oracle was rescaled to energy
   units. The global arms for the other three methods reproduce the first run
   exactly.
5. **Scope.** One-shot strategy selection against scripted defenders on a 7x7
   grid, not exact exploitability. The selector does not generalise well: the
   clean selector is materially suboptimal at roughly a fifth of held-out
   positions before any fault.
